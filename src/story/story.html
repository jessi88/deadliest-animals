What we fear is not the same as what kills us.

Our World in Data (OWID) estimates that animals are associated with roughly 1.5 million human deaths each year: around 920,000 from non-human animals and about 600,000 from direct violence and conflict among humans. Mosquitoes and snakes account for almost all of the non-human total. Although OWID presents its main chart as a 2023 comparison, the 21 values are not all direct counts from 2023. They are rounded annual estimates assembled from the best available evidence: many disease estimates use 2023 data, while several attack categories rely on multi-year averages or other recent records. The broad differences in magnitude are more reliable than the exact figures.

01 · The animals we notice

Fear has a familiar face.

Wolves. Sharks. Bears. Crocodiles. Their attacks are vivid enough to shape films, headlines and memory.

On their own scale, the differences are visible. But all of them sit below roughly 200 human deaths per year in OWID’s rounded annual estimates.

Hippos are a useful reminder: their total death toll is comparatively low, yet research cited by Our World in Data finds a very high fatality rate when encounters actually occur.

03 · The circle grows

The danger begins to change form.

Big cats. Bees and wasps. Elephants. Tsetse flies. Tapeworms. Scorpions. Roundworms.

The ranking moves away from the predators of our imagination and toward stings, parasites and infectious disease.

There is progress hidden here too. Recent estimates put deaths from sleeping sickness spread by tsetse flies near 1,000–1,500 a year, down from roughly 25,000 deaths per year around the year 2000.

04 · The pathway matters

Often, the animal is not the direct cause.

Sandflies transmit leishmaniasis. Kissing bugs spread Chagas disease. Freshwater snails are part of the life cycle of schistosomiasis.

Dogs reach a rounded estimate of about 40,000 human deaths per year — mostly from rabies after bites or scratches, not from attack injuries. At this point in the ranking, the animal is often a link in a chain of transmission.

05 · The second breakaway

The scale has to change again.

The chart opens onto a third scale: 0–800,000. Watch the animals we just saw travel into their new positions.

06 · Snakes

About 100,000 human deaths per year.

On the new scale, snakes are the first animal to become unmistakably large.

This is also one of the most uncertain estimates. The WHO gives a range of roughly 81,000 to 138,000 human deaths a year, and undercounting is plausible because many victims live in rural areas with limited healthcare and death registration.

07 · Then us

About 600,000 people killed by other humans per year.

One animal has been missing from the circle: us.

The estimate includes direct deaths from interpersonal violence, conflict and terrorism, and police conflict or execution. It does not include suicide or deaths from negligence such as road crashes.

08 · And then the mosquito

About 760,000 human deaths per year.

Mosquitoes extend farther still — not through physical strength, but by transmitting disease.

This category is the closest to a direct 2023 total: OWID’s disease-specific estimates sum to 755,987 deaths in 2023 and are rounded to 760,000. Malaria accounts for most of the total; dengue, Japanese encephalitis, yellow fever, chikungunya and other infections make up most of the rest.

The unsettling part is also the hopeful part: many of these deaths are preventable. Insecticide-treated bed nets, malaria treatment, dengue-control measures and snakebite antivenoms already exist. The gap is often access.

The most important part of the ranking

Deadly does not have to mean inevitable.

If mosquito deaths fell to zero, the estimated toll from non-human animals would be almost six times smaller. Remove snakebite deaths too, and the remainder would fall by almost another two-thirds.

Where lives can be saved

Some of the largest non-human killers are also the ones we know how to fight.

Insecticide-treated bed nets and other vector-control measures reduce malaria transmission. Early diagnosis and treatment prevent malaria deaths. Wolbachia programs are being used and evaluated to reduce dengue transmission. Antivenoms can save lives after snakebite. Rabies can be prevented through dog vaccination and prompt post-exposure care.

The harder problem is whether people can reach these tools when they need them. The ranking is therefore not only about fear. It is about exposure, access and prevention.
